For roughly fifty years — from the 1910s until television finished them off in
the 1960s — the newsreel was how moving pictures of the wider world reached
ordinary people. Sandwiched between the cartoon and the feature, a ten-minute
reel would race through coronations and floods, prize fights and politics, all
narrated in that unmistakable clipped, urgent voice. For most of the twentieth
century, this was the news as a moving image, and for many it was the only
moving image of distant events they would ever see.

Watched today, newsreels are a complicated, fascinating record. They are
genuine documents of real moments — the only film of events that would otherwise
survive only on paper. But they are also shaped, selected and spun: a newsreel
chose what a nation saw, decided the tone, and frequently served the interests
of the studios and governments that approved them. Learning to watch them is
partly learning to read between their confident lines.

### Reading the reel

Notice what gets the time and what gets ten seconds. Notice the music swelling
under the triumphs and the wry tone reserved for the human-interest closer — the
beauty pageant, the water-skiing squirrel, the reliable note of levity to send
the audience back to their seats. Notice, too, the silences: the stories a
newsreel of its era could not or would not tell.

Browsed together, these reels become something larger than any single bulletin:
a flickering, partial, endlessly revealing diary of how the twentieth century
narrated itself to itself, week by week, in the dark.
