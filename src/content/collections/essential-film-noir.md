Film noir was never a genre its makers set out to create. The term was coined
after the fact, by French critics who, when American thrillers finally reached
Paris screens after the war, noticed something dark and unified running through
them — a fatalism, a visual language of shadow, a moral weather system all its
own. What they named *film noir* was really a mood that had crept into Hollywood
crime pictures between roughly 1944 and 1958: low-key lighting borrowed from
German Expressionism, plots adapted from hard-boiled pulp, and a worldview in
which the deck is stacked and the house always wins.

Many of the finest examples have since slipped into the public domain, often
because their studios let copyrights lapse on pictures they considered
disposable B-movies. That accident of paperwork is our good fortune. It means
the gallery below — detectives narrating their own undoing, drifters lured by
the wrong woman, insurance men with larceny in their hearts — can be watched
freely today.

### What to look for

Noir rewards a particular kind of attention. Watch how the lighting does the
talking: a slatted shadow across a face like prison bars, a single desk lamp
carving a room into guilt and innocence. Listen for the voiceover, that
world-weary narration that so often tells you the hero is doomed before the
story has even begun. And notice the women — not villains exactly, but people
who have understood, faster than the men around them, exactly how the game is
played.

If you are new to the form, start with a tight, fast picture under ninety
minutes; noir is at its best when it is lean. Then work outward into the
stranger, slower entries, where the dread has time to settle. Either way, keep
the lights low. These films were built for the dark.
