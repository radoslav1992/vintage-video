In 1982, an archivist named Rick Prelinger began collecting a kind of film
almost nobody else thought worth saving: the *ephemeral* film. Not features, not
documentaries with ambitions, but the disposable reels of twentieth-century
life — educational shorts, industrial promos, public-service warnings,
advertising, the awkward classroom films about manners and hygiene that a
generation squirmed through. By the time the collection found a home at the
Internet Archive, it numbered in the tens of thousands.

Their value is something their makers never intended. A film teaching teenagers
how to behave on a date tells us, decades later, exactly what a society expected
of its young people — and of its girls especially. A cheerful reel selling a new
kitchen appliance is also a document of what a home was supposed to look like,
who was supposed to be standing in it, and what counted as progress. The
propaganda is unguarded precisely because it assumed no one would ever study it.

### How to watch the everyday

This collection is best browsed the way you might wander a flea market: without a
shopping list, open to the strange and the small. A film about traffic safety
becomes a time capsule of a vanished streetscape. An office-efficiency short
preserves the rhythms of work before the computer. The narration — always so
confident, always so sure of the future — is its own quiet comedy and its own
poignancy.

Watch a few in a row and a portrait assembles itself, almost against the films'
will: the hopes, the manners, the blind spots and the sales pitches of the
American century, captured by people who were only trying to sell a product or
make a point. The lasting record is the accident. The accident is the treasure.
